### Summary

**⏳ Time Travel or Social Media Scam?** Caro Claire Burke's *Yesteryear* is the most talked-about book of 2026. If you've finished this wild ride and are scratching your head over the ending, you're in the right place. We dive deep into Natalie’s narcissism, the 1855 "pioneer" reality, and what that final reveal actually means.

## The Premise: The Rise and Fall of Natalie

Natalie is a "tradwife" influencer with 8 million followers. She lives in a beautiful Idaho farmhouse, wears linen dresses, and pretends to churn butter the "old-fashioned" way. In reality, she has a fleet of nannies, a high-tech kitchen hidden behind faux-wood panels, and a husband who wouldn't know a plow from a pogo stick.

The book is a biting satire of the influencer industry—until the twist happens.

## The Twist: Waking Up in 1855

One morning, Natalie wakes up and her modern comforts are gone. She is in the year 1855. There are no Instagram filters, no indoor plumbing, and her six children are suddenly very real and very hungry.

The narrative splits into two timelines:
1.  **The Past:** Natalie’s struggle to survive in the brutal conditions of the 19th century.
2.  **The Present:** The events leading up to her "disappearance" from the modern world.

## Yesteryear Ending Explained: What Actually Happened?

The question every reader asks is: **Is Natalie actually in 1855?**

### The Three Theories
Throughout the book, Burke drops hints for three possible explanations:
*   **The Time Travel Theory:** A supernatural event triggered by Natalie’s obsession with the past.
*   **The Reality Show Theory:** A high-stakes, *Truman Show*-style intervention orchestrated by her fed-up family or a rival influencer.
*   **The Psychological Break Theory:** Natalie’s mind finally snapped under the pressure of maintaining her fake persona, and "1855" is a manifestation of her guilt and narcissism.

### The Final Reveal (Spoilers!)
The ending reveals that the "1855" timeline is a complex psychological simulation. Natalie’s husband, weary of the lies and the exploitation of their children, conspired with a high-end "rehabilitation" company that specializes in "extreme reality checks" for narcissists. 

However, the true "Burke Twist" is that Natalie doesn't learn a thing. Even when she realizes the simulation, her first thought isn't for her family—it's how she can market her "survival story" to get more followers.

## Why *Yesteryear* is the "Yellowface" of 2026

Much like R.F. Kuang’s *Yellowface*, *Yesteryear* features an abhorrent, unlikable protagonist. Natalie is cruel, selfish, and vain. Yet, you can’t stop reading. Burke masterfully makes us complicit in Natalie’s downfall, showing how our own hunger for "authentic" content fuels the very monsters we love to hate.

## Key Takeaways for Book Clubs

*   **The Hypocrisy of "Trad":** Discuss how Natalie uses modern technology to sell a vision of the past.
*   **Motherhood as Content:** How does Natalie view her children? (Hint: They are props).
*   **The Anne Hathaway Movie:** With a film adaptation in the works, how do you think they will handle the internal monologue of such an unlikable character?

## Conclusion

*Yesteryear* is a brilliant, uncomfortable, and addictive look at the cost of perfection. Whether you loved Natalie or hated her (and you probably hated her), the ending is a haunting reminder that in the world of social media, even the truth is just another product.
