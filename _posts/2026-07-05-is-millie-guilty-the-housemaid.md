### The Question at the Heart of the Novel

**Is Millie guilty?**

It is the question *The Housemaid* readers ask most often, and it is the question Freida McFadden is most deliberate about refusing to answer simply. This piece is a full examination of the moral, legal, and psychological dimensions of Millie's guilt — and of why the question itself is the point.

*This piece contains full spoilers for the ending of The Housemaid.*

---

## The Legal Answer

Let's start with the straightforward version: **legally, yes.** Millie Calloway kills Andrew Winchester. That is not ambiguous. She does it deliberately, with knowledge of what she is doing, and without a legal justification that would hold up in a conventional court.

If the novel's events were presented to a jury with no context — just the fact of the killing — Millie would likely be convicted of some degree of homicide.

That is the easy part. Now for the harder part.

---

## The Moral Answer

McFadden builds her case across the entire novel, and by the time Millie acts, the reader has access to information that a jury would not:

**1. Andrew is a serial predator.**
The revelation of what happened to Rachel — the housemaid before Millie — makes clear that Andrew has done this before. His method is practiced: identify a vulnerable woman, use charm and attention to create dependency, then use the power he holds over her (her housing, her employment, her social isolation) to trap her. Millie is not a unique victim. She is the latest in a pattern.

**2. No institution was going to protect her.**
Millie has a criminal record. Andrew has money, a professional reputation, and a house. In the calculus of who gets believed, she loses at every step. She knows this. The novel makes certain that the reader knows it too. The police, a lawyer, a social worker — none of these routes lead to Millie's safety. McFadden is not subtle about this. The novel's entire structure is built on showing you, methodically, that every door Millie might have turned to was already closed.

**3. Andrew would not have stopped.**
This is perhaps the most important point. The evidence the novel builds — through Rachel's story, through Nina's history, through Andrew's behavior toward Millie — establishes that Andrew has no internal mechanism for stopping. He is not a man who made mistakes and might reform. He is a man with a method who has refined it over years.

Given these three facts — pattern, institutional failure, no possibility of voluntary cessation — the moral question becomes: **what is the alternative?**

McFadden does not answer that question. She asks it.

---

## What Millie's Past Means for the Moral Calculus

Part of what makes *The Housemaid* so uncomfortable is that Millie is not a clean-hands protagonist. She has a criminal record. She has done things she is not proud of. McFadden deploys this history deliberately: it closes doors for Millie in the present story, and it also complicates easy reader sympathy.

If Millie were simply an innocent woman who stumbled into the Winchester household and was victimized by a monster, the moral question would have a simpler shape. But she is not simply that. She is a complicated person who has made bad choices and who is also, in the present moment, the victim of a systematic predator.

The novel asks you to hold both of those things at once — and to notice that courts rarely do.

---

## The Question the Novel Is Actually Asking

"Is Millie guilty?" seems like a question about Millie. It is actually a question about systems.

McFadden is asking: **in a world where the institutions meant to protect people like Millie systematically fail them, and where predators like Andrew use their social position as armor — what does justice actually look like?**

The novel's answer is not triumphant. It is not a celebration of vigilante justice or a simple statement that what Millie does is right. The killing is violent and final and messy. McFadden does not aestheticize it.

What the novel does, instead, is sit with the reality that justice — formal, legal, institutional justice — was never going to arrive in the Winchester house. And then it asks you what you think should have happened instead.

---

## Why Readers Keep Asking the Question

The fact that readers and film audiences keep returning to "Is Millie guilty?" is not just a sign that the ending is shocking. It is a sign that McFadden has done her job: she has written a novel that makes the question genuinely hard.

We are conditioned to want narrative closure that aligns with legal categories. Guilty or not guilty. Justified or unjustified. Good person or bad person. *The Housemaid* declines to give you that closure — and the discomfort you feel as a result is the novel working exactly as intended.

**Millie killed someone.** That is true.

**Andrew would have destroyed her, and Rachel, and Nina, and whoever came next.** That is also true.

The novel holds both of these truths without resolving the tension between them. The reader has to live in that tension — which is, perhaps, closer to how justice actually works (and fails) in the real world than any tidy verdict could be.

---

## Our Answer

If you pressed us: **morally, Millie is not guilty in any sense that matters.**

She ended the predation of a man who had systematically destroyed multiple women, who had no intention of stopping, and whom no institution was going to touch. She did so with the full knowledge of what she was doing and why. In a world with functional alternatives, she might have made a different choice. The novel is, in part, about the fact that she did not live in that world.

But we hold that answer loosely — because McFadden wrote a novel precise enough to make us uncertain, and that uncertainty is the point.
