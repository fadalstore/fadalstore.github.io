### Quick Answer

The 2025 *Housemaid* film with **Sydney Sweeney** and **Amanda Seyfried** is a faithful adaptation in spirit but makes meaningful structural changes — most notably in pacing, some character backstory, and the handling of Rachel's story. The book is psychologically richer; the film is more viscerally immediate. Both are worth your time.

## What the Film Gets Right

### The Atmosphere

The Winchester house is one of the most important "characters" in McFadden's novel. Its polished surfaces, its locked rooms, its sense of spaces that don't quite want you in them — the film captures all of this. The visual language of wealth concealing rot translates powerfully to the screen.

### Sydney Sweeney as Millie

Sweeney's casting is one of the film's genuine strengths. Millie on the page is a woman who carries her past like a stone — you can feel its weight in every decision she makes, every interaction she manages. Sweeney brings that quality without overplaying it. Her Millie is watchful, controlled, and quietly dangerous long before the film reveals why.

### Amanda Seyfried as Nina

Nina is the harder role. On the page, she reads as the novel's apparent antagonist for most of its length, and she has to be genuinely unpleasant without tipping her hand too early. Seyfried threads this needle well — her Nina is hostile and brittle in ways that feel real rather than theatrical, which makes the eventual reveal land with the weight it deserves.

### The Ending's Emotional Core

Without spoiling the specific changes the film makes, the emotional logic of the ending — the alliance between Millie and Nina, the finality of what happens to Andrew — is preserved and delivered with real impact. Audiences who came out of the film wanting to talk about it the way readers talk about the book is the highest praise an adaptation can receive.

## Key Differences: Movie vs. Book

### 1. Pacing and Revelation

**In the book:** McFadden builds her psychological trap slowly. Andrew's predatory nature is revealed gradually, through small accumulations of wrongness. The reader is kept in genuine uncertainty about who to trust for a long time.

**In the film:** The compressed runtime (and the demands of visual storytelling) mean the dread escalates more quickly. Some of the slow-burn uncertainty that makes the book so unsettling is traded for a more direct thriller momentum.

**Verdict:** Book wins for psychological texture; film wins for immediate tension.

### 2. Millie's Backstory

**In the book:** Millie's past — what she did, why she has a record, what it costs her in the present — is unfolded carefully and with some ambiguity. You know she is not innocent before the law; you don't immediately know what that means about who she is.

**In the film:** The backstory is handled differently, with some elements compressed or resequenced. The film makes certain choices about how much of Millie's history to present and when, which changes the texture of her moral complexity.

### 3. Rachel's Story

**In the book:** Rachel — the housemaid who preceded Millie — is a ghost who haunts the narrative. Her story is revealed in pieces, and the full picture of what happened to her is one of the novel's most disturbing sequences.

**In the film:** Rachel's story is handled with different emphasis and some structural changes. The revelation of her fate hits with emotional force in both versions, but the specific details and the way that information is delivered differ meaningfully.

### 4. Nina's Inner Life

**In the book:** McFadden gives Nina her own chapters — in a past tense that slowly reveals what her experience of life with Andrew has actually been. This dual-timeline structure is one of the novel's most effective devices.

**In the film:** A single-timeline visual story means Nina's perspective is conveyed through performance rather than direct access to her thoughts. Seyfried's work carries this weight, but readers of the novel will miss the intimacy of Nina's own voice.

### 5. The Final Act

**In the book:** The final confrontation is drawn out with psychological precision. You understand, in real time, what each character is thinking and why.

**In the film:** The climax is more kinetic — as film climaxes tend to be. The visual medium demands a different kind of energy in its final sequences, and the film delivers accordingly.

## Which Is Better?

This is the question every adaptation forces, and the honest answer is: **they are different experiences, not better or worse.**

**Read the book if:**
- You want full access to every character's psychology
- You want to catch all the clues McFadden hid in plain sight
- You want to understand, completely, why Millie does what she does

**Watch the film if:**
- You want the emotional punch of the story delivered quickly and viscerally
- You want to see Sydney Sweeney and Amanda Seyfried inhabit these roles
- You already read the book and want to see it come to life

**Our recommendation:** Read the book first. Then watch the film and appreciate what it chose to keep, what it changed, and why. The conversation between the two versions is more interesting than either alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the movie have the same ending as the book?
The emotional outcome is the same — Andrew meets justice, Millie and Nina are aligned — but the specific staging and some surrounding details differ. The film's ending is effective on its own terms while staying true to the novel's moral logic.

### Is the film as good as the book?
They are different experiences. The film is a strong, well-cast adaptation that captures the story's emotional core. The book is richer psychologically. Most readers who loved the book will appreciate the film; most viewers who saw the film first will find the book rewards them with more depth.

### Who is in The Housemaid movie?
Sydney Sweeney plays Millie Calloway and Amanda Seyfried plays Nina Winchester. The film was released December 19, 2025, and grossed over $400 million worldwide.

### Will there be a Housemaid 2 movie?
A sequel has been confirmed following the film's commercial success. Given that McFadden has written *The Housemaid's Secret* (the book sequel), source material exists for a film continuation.
